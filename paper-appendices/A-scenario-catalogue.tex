\section{Scenario Catalogue and ATT\&CK Mapping}
\label{app:scenarios}

This appendix specifies the five behaviour classes that were scripted against the
laboratory testbed. Three are malicious, executed by an identity that Teleport had
already authenticated and authorised. Two are benign: one representing routine
traffic and one representing legitimate activity that is designed to trip a
detection rule, so that the evaluation has a genuine negative class.

\subsection{Synthetic database schema}
\label{app:scenarios:schema}

All activity ran against a single PostgreSQL~16 database, \code{procurement},
holding a synthetic public-procurement schema in the \code{public} schema. No real
records were used and no field was derived from any live system; every name,
identifier and bank account is generated. Table~\ref{tab:app-schema} gives the tables
as they exist in the running testbed, with seeded row counts read from the
initialisation script rather than from the design document. A further table,
\code{bids\_archive}, was seeded as a target for destructive statements.

These are the counts at seeding. The corpus run changes them, which is the point of
running it: after the fourteen days, \code{payments} holds 8,337 rows because the
approved retention job in class~B1 deleted the rest, and \code{bids\_archive} no
longer exists because a class~S3 statement dropped it. Any replication must reseed
before comparing.

{\sloppy Two views carry the access-control design that makes the attacks detectable.
\code{public.employees\_public} exposes \code{employees} without \code{salary},
\code{national\_id} or \code{bank\_account}; \code{public.bids\_public} exposes
\code{bids} only for rows whose \code{sealed\_until} has passed. The reporting role is
granted the views and not the base tables, so a read of \code{employees} or
\code{bids} by that role is itself the signal rather than merely a large query.\par}

Four database roles were defined, mirroring a conventional role-based access
control deployment: \code{app\_service} (the application's connection pool),
\code{analyst\_ro} (read-only reporting), \code{proc\_officer} (read and write on
tenders and bids) and \code{dba} (administrative). Teleport mapped each human user
to exactly one of the three human roles through its own role definitions, so the
database role in an audit event is attributable to a named person.

Class S3 replaces the phrase ``insider SQL injection'' used in the research
proposal. An insider who reaches the database through an identity-aware proxy holds
valid credentials and a valid session, so the defining feature of SQL injection,
namely subverting an application's input handling to reach the database at all, is
absent. What remains, and what actually threatens the data, is destructive or
deliberately obfuscated SQL issued directly by an authorised principal. The
detection problem and the remediation problem both change accordingly, and
Section~\ref{sec:method} adopts the corrected framing throughout.

Statements are unevenly distributed across the four database roles, in proportion to
how each role is used: \code{app\_service} issued \num{33492} statements,
\code{analyst\_ro} \num{8681}, \code{proc\_officer} \num{4725} and \code{dba}

represented in class S3, which is the asymmetry the detection rules exploit.

Sixteen of the \num{25} steps that raised no alert are reconnaissance:
\code{SELECT current\_user}, \code{SELECT count(*)} and reads of \code{pg\_roles}. They
are indistinguishable from ordinary administrative curiosity at the statement level and
were deliberately left uncovered, because a rule broad enough to catch them would fire on
routine traffic and destroy the false positive rate reported in
Appendix~\ref{app:rules}. The other nine are substantive and their omission is a genuine
gap: four paginated exports of \code{salary}, \code{national\_id} and
\code{bank\_account} that each returned 300 rows and so fell under the 500-row threshold
of \code{PG-EXF-001}, two twelve-thousand-row reads of \code{payments}, one
\code{ALTER ROLE ... NOSUPERUSER}, and two \code{DROP OWNED BY ...; DROP ROLE} chains.
Session-level recall is nevertheless \num{12} of \num{12}: every malicious session was
caught by at least one of its steps.
